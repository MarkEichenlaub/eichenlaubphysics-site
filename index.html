About Mark

I'm always impressed by the creativity and resilience students show when they have a great problem to work on. Competition physics is full of great problems, and it's where I've spent most of my career.

From 2016 to 2021 I coached the US Physics Team, including as team leader at the International Physics Olympiad. I've helped write the F=ma and USAPhO exams and graded USAPhO. I bring that experience to preparing my students.

Since 2018 I've been a professional physics curriculum developer. I designed a well-known online olympiad training course, and I now lead a team writing physics courses that run from middle school through olympiad preparation.

My PhD at the University of Maryland was in physics education research. My thesis was about how students learn to use math to make sense of physical phenomena: when an equation means something to them, and when they're only moving symbols around. While I was there, I taught and helped design the introductory physics course for life-science majors.

I've been teaching physics since I was an undergraduate at Caltech. I've designed and taught physics and astronomy courses at Bucknell University, Johns Hopkins CTY, and the Stanford Pre-Collegiate Summer Institutes, and I've tutored private students from middle schoolers to adults.

I was also active for years answering physics questions online. You can read my answers on my Physics Stack Exchange profile and my Quora profile.

How I teach

  1. Physical meaning first

    Even when a student has the math to do a problem, I start as close to the real phenomenon as I can, so physics doesn't turn into manipulating formulas. We look at extreme cases and check units before the heavy math, and we check the answer against our intuition after.

  2. You do the reasoning

    Most of a session is me asking questions and you figuring out the answers. When you're stuck, I give the smallest hint that gets you moving again. One of the best signs of progress is when you start catching your own mistakes.

  3. Judgment, not only technique

    Hard problems take judgment: what belongs in the model, how long to stay on a problem, and whether there's a shorter way than bashing through. When you get a problem right but it took a long time, we go back and find the shortcut together.

  4. Every problem connects

    Each problem we do is saved and tagged. When a technique shows up again in a new setting, we pull up the old problem and see how they're related.

  5. Honest about difficulty

    I won't tell you something is easy. Physics is hard, and when something that made sense in the textbook gets confusing on a hard problem, that's okay. It means you're learning.

  6. A plan with dates on it

    Each student has a written plan with goals tied to the calendar, like the F=ma exam in February. We measure what we've covered against it, I tell you if we start falling behind, and I change it as I learn what you need.

Questions

Are sessions online?

Yes. We meet on Google Meet and work on a shared whiteboard in the portal. A tablet helps, since we'll want to write equations and draw diagrams on the fly, but it isn't required.

How often should we meet?

I usually recommend one or two sessions a week. Before a contest, some students meet more often.

What does it cost?

$3,000 for a ten-session cycle. This includes ten hour-long one-on-one meetings, custom homework assignments between meetings, mock exams, my feedback on your work before each session, and a detailed report each cycle on our progress and goals for the upcoming cycle. It also includes unlimited questions by email between sessions. The half-hour intro session is free.

What if we need to cancel or reschedule?

Just let me know, or move the session yourself in the portal. I try to be as flexible as I can about cancellations, time changes, and extra sessions, since most students are balancing a lot of activities.

What happens in the intro session?

I like to hear about the student: their background, what interests them, and their goals. I'll answer your questions about how I work. If the student can join, I'm happy to meet them too.

When should a student start preparing for F=ma?

For students who put in a real study effort, it's typical to come close to passing the F=ma exam as a freshman, and to pass it and get invited to USAPhO as a sophomore. Some freshmen pass it, too.

Do I need to be at olympiad level already?

No. Some students come in with a contest in mind, and some just like physics and want more of it than school gives them. We start where you are.
