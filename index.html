About Mark

I've been teaching physics since I was an undergraduate at Caltech. I've designed and taught physics and astronomy courses at Bucknell University, Johns Hopkins CTY, and the Stanford Pre-Collegiate Summer Institutes, and I've tutored private students from middle schoolers to adults studying for the MCAT.

My PhD at the University of Maryland was in physics education research. My thesis was about how students learn to use math to make sense of physical phenomena: when an equation means something to them, and when they're only moving symbols around. While I was there, I taught and helped design the introductory physics course for life-science majors.

From 2016 to 2021 I coached the US Physics Team, including as team leader at the International Physics Olympiad. I've helped write the F=ma and USAPhO exams and graded USAPhO, so I know what each medal level takes.

In 2018 I became the first physics curriculum developer at Art of Problem Solving, where I designed PhysicsWOOT, their olympiad course. I now lead their physics curriculum, from Middle School Physics through PhysicsWOOT. I've also written about 450 answers on Physics Stack Exchange, where I'm one of the site's top 40 users.

I'm always impressed by the creativity and resilience students show when they have a great problem to work on.

How I teach

Calculation in the service of intuition

  1. Physical meaning first

    Even when a student has the math to do a problem, I start as close to the real phenomenon as I can, so physics doesn't turn into manipulating formulas. We look at extreme cases and check units before the heavy math, and we check the answer against our intuition after.

  2. You do the reasoning

    Most of a session is me asking questions and you figuring out the answers. When you're stuck, I give the smallest hint that gets you moving again. One of the best signs of progress is when you start catching your own mistakes.

  3. Judgment, not only technique

    Hard problems take judgment: what belongs in the model, how long to stay on a problem, and whether there's a shorter way than bashing through. When you get a problem right but it took a long time, we go back and find the shortcut together.

  4. Every problem connects

    Each problem we do is saved and tagged. When a technique shows up again in a new setting, we pull up the old problem and see how they're related.

  5. Honest about difficulty

    I won't tell you something is easy. Physics is hard, and when something that made sense in the textbook gets confusing on a hard problem, that's okay. It means you're learning.

  6. A plan with dates on it

    Each student has a written plan with goals tied to the calendar, like the F=ma exam in February. We measure what we've covered against it, I tell you if we start falling behind, and I change it as I learn what you need.

What you get

Sessions, plus everything between them

I work as a teacher more than a tutor. Besides helping with problems, I keep the schedule, assign work, track your progress, and make sure you know the format of each exam and how to sign up for it.

Live sessions

One-on-one over Google Meet, usually an hour, on a shared whiteboard built for physics: typed and handwritten math, graphs, and calculators. It works on a laptop or an iPad.

A record of every session

After each session, a written summary and the whole whiteboard as a PDF go into your portal, so you can go back to any problem we worked.

Problems picked for you

About 7,000 problems in the portal: F=ma, USAPhO, IPhO, EuPhO, national olympiads from around the world, and textbooks like Morin. Olympiad problems come with IPhO-style answer sheets.

Homework and questions

You get homework picked for you after each session. You upload your work in the portal, and I read it, along with any questions you email me, before we meet again.

Practice exams

Timed F=ma and USAPhO practice exams in the portal, with a question-by-question report we go over together.

Daily fluency drills

Five minutes a day of spaced practice on the formulas and math you need at your fingertips, so they don't slow you down on a contest.

Handouts written for you

When a topic needs more than a problem set, I write a handout on it, like matrices for olympiad physics or units inside logarithms.

A report every ten sessions

At the end of each block of ten sessions, we look back together and I write a report: our goals going in, what we did, and the goals for the next block.

Parents in the loop

Parents get their own portal login with the plan, session summaries, reports, and invoices, and they can book a check-in call with me.

Questions

Are sessions online?

Yes. We meet on Google Meet and work on a shared whiteboard in the portal. A tablet helps, since we'll want to write equations and draw diagrams on the fly, but it isn't required.

How often should we meet?

I usually recommend one or two sessions a week. Before a contest, some students meet more often.

What does it cost?

$300 an hour. Most families prepay ten hours at a time, and each session draws on that balance for as long as it runs. That covers my prep, reading your homework before each session, the session summaries, and the progress report at the end of each block. The intro session is free.

What if we need to cancel or reschedule?

Just let me know, or move the session yourself in the portal. I try to be as flexible as I can about cancellations, time changes, and extra sessions, since most students are balancing a lot of activities.

What happens in the intro session?

I like to hear about the student: their background, what interests them, and their goals. I'll answer your questions about how I work. If the student can join, I'm happy to meet them too.

When should a student start preparing for F=ma?

For students who put in a real study effort, it's typical to come close to passing the F=ma exam as a freshman, and to pass it and get invited to USAPhO as a sophomore. Some freshmen pass it, too.

Do I need to be at olympiad level already?

No. Some students come in with a contest in mind, and some just like physics and want more of it than school gives them. We start where you are.
